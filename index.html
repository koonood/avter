Import guide

Bring your conversation with you.

Choose the platform where your messages live. We'll show you how to export them and what to upload.

WhatsApp Android · iPhone · WhatsApp Web
+

Android

  1. Open the conversation in WhatsApp.
  2. Tap ⋮ → More → Export chat.
  3. Choose Without media for a smaller export.
  4. Save or send the exported file to your computer.

iPhone

  1. Open the conversation in WhatsApp.
  2. Tap the contact or group name at the top.
  3. Choose Export Chat.
  4. Choose Without Media and save the ZIP.
Upload: .txt or .zip
WhatsApp Web and Desktop are useful for viewing messages, but the native export is done from your phone.
Telegram Windows · macOS · Linux
+

Telegram Desktop

  1. Install and open Telegram Desktop.
  2. Open the chat and use ⋮ → Export chat history.
  3. For a wider export, open Settings → Advanced → Export Telegram Data.
  4. Select Machine-readable JSON when available.
Upload: .json or .zip
Telegram's desktop app provides the most practical native export workflow.
Instagram DMs iPhone · Android · Web
+

Meta Accounts Center

  1. Open Instagram → Settings and activity → Accounts Center.
  2. Open Your information and permissions → Download your information.
  3. Select your Instagram account and request a download.
  4. Choose JSON when available and request the archive.
  5. Download the archive and upload it here.
Upload: .zip / JSON
The exact menu names may vary slightly depending on your Instagram version and language.
Messenger iPhone · Android · Web

Meta Accounts Center

  1. Open Facebook or Instagram and go to Accounts Center.

  3. Select your Facebook/Messenger information.
  4. Choose JSON and request the download.
  5. Download the archive and upload it here.

The questions that actually matter

So… why did this relationship end?

After turns the evidence into clear, honest answers to the questions people can't stop asking after a breakup.

Not one fight or one message: reciprocity dropped, conflicts were repaired less often and plans became vaguer — together, over months.
After gives two dates: the earliest repeated signal and the strongest structural shift. It shows when communication changed, not the private moment when feelings did.
Distance and shorter replies fit many explanations. Without direct evidence in the archive, After treats it as a hypothesis — never a fact.
The useful answer is usually neither yes nor no: which loops did you both feed, and what was outside your control? No fake blame percentages.
The report marks windows: an early one when the pattern was new, a repair window when signals converged, and a late one where reversal was unlikely.
Connection and decline can coexist for months. A timeline shows that balance instead of hunting for one final red flag.
Your own recurring pattern: what changed, how long it lasted and whether repair worked — so you can recognise the loop sooner next time.
FAQ

Questions you might have.

Do we have to have lived together?

No. The analysis uses only your communication history, so long-distance and weekend relationships work too.

What if we didn't text much?

Sparse periods stay in the timeline, with lower confidence when the evidence is thin.

Can it tell when someone stopped loving me?

No — private feelings can't be read from text. It shows when observable patterns changed and separates observation from interpretation.

Will I see the actual messages behind a conclusion?

That's the goal: every major finding links to a period and selected source messages.

This prototype demonstrates the product experience. A production version would process the export on a secure backend, keep evidence links to source messages and clearly separate observed patterns from interpretation. It cannot know another person's private feelings from text alone.
